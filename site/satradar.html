How this works

Every 15 minutes EUMETSAT's Meteosat satellite over the Indian Ocean scans the region, and EUMETSAT's H SAF service turns the infrared and microwave readings into a rain rate (product H63). This page reads those pictures directly, so it refreshes on its own every 15 minutes while open. Each new picture arrives about 45–55 minutes after the scan.

Satellite rain over Tamil Nadu is usually too high, because the cold cloud shields of the monsoon look like rain from space. Compared with the 565 TN SMART gauges on 10–15 Oct 2026, it gave about 3 to 5 times the measured rain, so the page corrects it. The correction is checked every 30 minutes against IMD's Chennai and Karaikal radars (when they are working) and against the TN SMART gauges; the radar pictures themselves are not shown here.

Forecast frames (purple label) move the latest rain along its recent track: the page compares pictures 30 minutes apart, works out how each part of the rain is moving, and carries each rain cell forward in its own direction and speed (the arrows). That track is blended with the winds 1.5–5 km up from the latest GFS model run, which steer the clouds. When the satellite track flips between pictures or disagrees with those winds (common on afternoons with weak winds, when storms grow and collapse faster than they move), the cell follows the winds and its arrow is dashed orange and marked "direction uncertain". Rain that forms or dies in the meantime is not predicted, so trust the first hour more than the second. South India looks 4 hours ahead across the peninsula and the Bay of Bengal. Long range covers both seas and looks 6 hours ahead using pictures 30 minutes apart; after 3 hours its forecast is shown faded, as a rough guide to direction and timing only. Short range uses the same satellite pictures zoomed in, so it is good for timing but each square is still about 3–4 km across. "Now" is itself a forecast, because the newest picture is already 45–55 minutes old.
